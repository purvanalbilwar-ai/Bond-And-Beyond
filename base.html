Acids & bases · the base side

What makes a base?

Chemists describe bases in different ways. Learn the three major definitions, then see how base strength is classified.

A base can accept a proton

In this reaction, ammonia accepts H⁺ from water and forms ammonium and hydroxide ions.

NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻Ammonia acts as a Brønsted–Lowry base

Three definitions of a base

Each definition describes basic behaviour from a different point of view.

💧

Arrhenius base

In water

An Arrhenius base increases the concentration of hydroxide ions, OH⁻, when dissolved in water.

Example: Sodium hydroxide dissolves into ions:
NaOH → Na⁺ + OH⁻
🔁

Brønsted–Lowry base

Proton acceptor

A Brønsted–Lowry base accepts a proton (H⁺) from another substance.

Example: Ammonia accepts H⁺ from water:
NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻
••

Lewis base

Electron pair donor

A Lewis base donates a pair of electrons to form a bond. This definition includes reactions without proton transfer.

Example: Ammonia donates its lone pair to boron trifluoride:
NH₃ + BF₃ → H₃N→BF₃
⚡

Strong and weak bases

Extent of reaction

Strong bases ionise or dissociate almost completely in water. Weak bases react only partially and establish an equilibrium.

Strong: NaOH → Na⁺ + OH⁻
Weak: NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻
Remember: Strong vs weak describes how much a base reacts in water. Concentrated vs dilute describes how much base is present in a given volume; these are different ideas.

The definitions are related, but each one focuses on a different part of a reaction.

Base or alkali?

A base neutralises an acid. An alkali is a base that dissolves in water and produces OH⁻ ions. So, all alkalis are bases, but some bases do not dissolve well in water.

Neutralisation

When an acid reacts with a base, they can form a salt and water. The net ionic equation is:

H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂OHCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O

Amphoteric substances

Some substances can react as either an acid or a base, depending on what they react with. Water is one example; aluminium oxide and zinc oxide are amphoteric oxides.

Base strength and pH

A stronger base produces more OH⁻ in water at the same concentration. For dilute aqueous solutions at 25°C, pH + pOH = 14. The pH page shows where solutions sit on the scale.

Examples from school chemistry

These examples are commonly studied in school chemistry.

🧪

Sodium hydroxide

Formula: NaOH · a strong Arrhenius base; it dissociates in water to give OH⁻ ions.

🧪

Potassium hydroxide

Formula: KOH · a strong Arrhenius base that dissociates in water.

⚗️

Ammonia

Formula: NH₃ · a weak Brønsted–Lowry base; it accepts H⁺ from water.

⚗️

Methylamine

Formula: CH₃NH₂ · a weak Brønsted–Lowry base that accepts a proton.

Bases in everyday products

Bases also have useful roles in manufacturing, construction, and medicine.

🧼

Soap making

Sodium hydroxide is used to make many solid soaps through a process called saponification.

🏗️

Building materials

Calcium hydroxide, Ca(OH)₂, is used in mortar and plaster, and to reduce soil acidity.

💊

Antacids

Magnesium hydroxide, Mg(OH)₂, is used in some antacid products to neutralise excess stomach acid.

🌱

Fertilisers

Ammonia, NH₃, is an important starting material for making nitrogen fertilisers.

🧽

Cleaning products

Some cleaning products contain alkaline substances that help remove grease. Their ingredients vary.

🧪

Water treatment

Calcium hydroxide can be used in water treatment to adjust pH and help remove impurities.

🥨

Baking and cooking

Sodium hydrogen carbonate, NaHCO₃, is used in baking powder and some recipes to release carbon dioxide and help dough rise.

🫧

Water softening

Sodium carbonate, Na₂CO₃, is used in some processes to soften water by helping remove calcium ions.

Safety: Some bases, including concentrated sodium hydroxide, potassium hydroxide, and ammonia solutions, can cause serious injury. Never handle them without teacher supervision and proper lab safety equipment.
