What is an Atom?

An atom is the smallest unit of an element that retains its chemical properties. Everything around us, including air, water, plants, animals and human beings, is made up of atoms. An atom consists of three main subatomic particles:

Protons (+), Neutrons (0) and Electrons (-).

Protons and neutrons are present inside the nucleus, while electrons occupy regions around the nucleus called orbitals or electron shells.

Three Main Subatomic Particles

🔴

Proton

A proton is a positively charged particle found inside the nucleus of an atom.

Symbol: p⁺
Charge: Positive (+1)
Location: Nucleus

🟣

Neutron

A neutron is a neutral particle found inside the nucleus of an atom.

Symbol: n⁰
Charge: Zero (0)
Location: Nucleus

🔵

Electron

An electron is a negatively charged particle found outside the nucleus.

Symbol: e⁻
Charge: Negative (-1)
Location: Around nucleus

Important Atomic Theories and Models

1. Dalton's Atomic Theory (1803)

John Dalton proposed his atomic theory in the early nineteenth century.

Main Postulates:
1. All matter is made up of tiny particles called atoms.
2. Atoms of the same element were considered identical in mass and properties.
3. Atoms of different elements have different properties.
4. Atoms combine in simple whole-number ratios to form compounds.
5. Chemical reactions involve rearrangement of atoms.

Model: Solid Sphere Model.
Limitation: Atoms are divisible into subatomic particles, and isotopes of an element can have different masses.

2. J.J. Thomson's Atomic Model (1904)

J.J. Thomson discovered the electron in 1897 through cathode ray experiments.

He proposed the Plum Pudding Model of the atom.

Main Ideas:
1. An atom is a positively charged sphere.
2. Negatively charged electrons are embedded inside this sphere.
3. The total positive and negative charges balance each other.

Model: Plum Pudding Model.
Limitation: It could not explain the results of Rutherford's alpha-particle scattering experiment.

3. Rutherford's Nuclear Model (1911)

Ernest Rutherford conducted the famous Gold Foil Experiment.

He directed alpha particles towards a thin sheet of gold foil.

Observations:
1. Most alpha particles passed straight through.
2. Some particles were deflected.
3. Very few particles bounced back.

Conclusions:
1. Most of the atom is empty space.
2. Positive charge is concentrated in a tiny central nucleus.
3. Electrons occupy the region around the nucleus.

Model: Nuclear Model.
Limitation: It could not explain the stability of atoms or their line spectra.

4. James Chadwick's Discovery (1932)

James Chadwick discovered the neutron in 1932.

He bombarded beryllium with alpha particles and observed a penetrating radiation.

This radiation consisted of neutral particles with a mass approximately equal to that of a proton.

Conclusions:
1. The nucleus contains protons and neutrons.
2. Neutrons have no electric charge.
3. Neutrons contribute significantly to the mass of an atom.

Discovery: Neutron (1932).

History of Atomic Discoveries

1803 – John Dalton

Proposed modern atomic theory.

1897 – J.J. Thomson

Discovered the electron.

1911 – Ernest Rutherford

Proposed the nuclear model of the atom.

1917–1919 – Rutherford

Identified the hydrogen nucleus as a fundamental nuclear particle, later called the proton.

1932 – James Chadwick

Discovered the neutron.

Conclusion

Atoms are the fundamental building blocks of ordinary matter.

Protons, neutrons and electrons help us understand the structure and properties of atoms.

The discoveries of Dalton, Thomson, Rutherford and Chadwick transformed our understanding of atomic science.
